Why it's built this way

  1. 1
    The balance never hides.

    A wallet chip lives in the header on every page — balance plus days remaining, readable at arm's length. Seniors shouldn't dig into an account page to learn what they can spend; today most plan portals bury it, which is a big reason ~$5B of OTC dollars go unused each year.

  2. 2
    Ownership first, countdown second.

    The greeting frames the money as theirs and meant to be used ("your plan set aside $300 — it's yours"). The deadline is a calendar fact with a real date, styled in amber, never red: urgency without alarm, and no shame about what's already spent.

  3. 3
    One primary action per state.

    Every wallet state resolves to a single verb — here, "Put my $184 to work." Not four competing buttons. The dollar amount lives inside the button so the action and the stakes are one sentence.

  4. 4
    "$0 for you" is its own message, not a product detail.

    Items the plan covers outside the allowance (DME-rail items like compression stockings) get a dedicated green strip. It protects the $300 from being wasted on things that were already free — the quiet core of benefit optimization.

  5. 5
    Purses connect to goals, not categories.

    Suggestions are framed by what Margaret said she's trying to accomplish ("staying steady at home"), each item badged with the purse that pays for it. Badges speak benefit language — "Home Safety · $24" — never plan jargon like "SSBCI" or "coinsurance."

  6. 6
    Show money working, not just remaining.

    "$76 is already working for you in Friday's order" reframes spent dollars as progress. A wallet that only counts down feels like loss; one that shows value arriving feels like a plan being used well.

  7. 7
    Before-and-after at the moment of commitment.

    The cart answers the question every member silently asks: "what will I have left?" One bar shows used / this order / still available, plus the saved size from her Fit Profile — typed once, applied everywhere.
